Fractional Real Estate & Crowdfunding Deals

Alternative Investments Intermediate 4 min read

The $100 building, honestly examined

Crowdfunding platforms promise the previously impossible: a slice of an apartment complex for the price of dinner. Since the 2012 JOBS Act legalized online private offerings, platforms like Fundrise, RealtyMogul, EquityMultiple and (infamously) dozens of defunct competitors have raised billions from retail investors. Some of it built real wealth. Some of it built the sponsors' beach houses. This module is about telling those apart — using the capital-stack and fee tools you'll sharpen further in the advanced tier.

First, know what you're actually buying. It is never "a building." It's one of three securities: equity in an LLC that owns one property (highest risk/reward, 5–10 year lock), a debt note the platform originated against a property (fixed 8–11%, shorter, capped upside), or shares of an eREIT/fund — a diversified pool with quarterly-at-best liquidity. Same website, wildly different risk shapes.

The three questions that filter 90% of deals

One: who is the sponsor and what is their track record across a full cycle? The platform is a shopping mall; the sponsor is the restaurant. Demand named principals, deals they ran through 2008 or 2020, and realized (not projected) returns. First-fund sponsors offering 20% IRRs to strangers on the internet are learning with your tuition. Two: where am I in the capital stack? "Preferred equity paying 8%" sits behind the bank's 65% LTV loan — a 20% value drop can vaporize you while the marketing said "preferred." Three: what are the total fees? Platform fee (0.5–1.5%), sponsor acquisition fee (1–2% of the whole purchase price, not your slice), asset management (1–2%/yr), disposition fee, promote. Stacked, 3–5% annually is common — meaning a deal must gross ~13% for you to net ~9%.

Fee Autopsy

$50,000 into a 5-year equity deal: 1% platform + 2% acquisition (your share ≈ $2,400 upfront) + 1.5%/yr asset mgmt ($3,750 cumulative) + sponsor promote of 20% over an 8% pref. Gross deal IRR 14% → your net ≈ 9.5%. Not theft — but a third of the return went to intermediaries, and a public REIT index charging 0.1% historically returned within shouting distance of that net, with daily liquidity.

Illiquidity is a feature and a sentence

Public REITs reprice every second; crowdfunded equity reprices when the sponsor says so. That smoothness feels pleasant — your statement never shows a crash — but the exit rules are the fine print that matters. Single-deal equity returns capital when the property sells, full stop; "targeted hold: 5 years" routinely becomes 8. eREITs offer redemption programs that are explicitly suspendable — Fundrise gated redemptions in 2020, BREIT (the $60B institutional cousin) limited withdrawals for over a year starting late 2022. None of this is scandal; it's structure. Money you might need before the hold ends does not belong in these vehicles at any advertised return.

Sizing and Selection

If you allocate here: cap the sleeve at 5–10% of investable assets, spread it across 4–5 deals/sponsors rather than one, prefer debt notes or diversified funds for your first exposure, and underwrite the sponsor harder than the building. The advanced modules (capital stack, lending, waterfalls) turn you into exactly the investor these platforms find hardest to sell to — read them before your first $1,000, not after.

There is no wrong row — only wrong matches between row and investor. The pattern to avoid is paying private-deal fees for public-market performance, which is the crowdfunding industry's median outcome. The pattern to seek is a proven sponsor, a defensible position in the stack, fees under 2% equivalent, and a hold you can genuinely forget about.
