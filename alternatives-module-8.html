Analyzing REITs Like a Professional

Alternative Investments Intermediate 4 min read

Why REIT earnings are fake (legally)

Look up any REIT's P/E ratio and you'll see something absurd — 60, 80, sometimes no earnings at all — for a company collecting rent from a thousand tenants. The culprit is depreciation: accounting rules force REITs to pretend their buildings lose value on a fixed schedule, even as those buildings appreciate in the real world. A REIT can collect $500M of rent, pay $350M of real expenses, and report near-zero "earnings" after a $140M non-cash depreciation charge. P/E is useless here. The industry's fix is FFO — funds from operations: net income with depreciation added back and one-time property-sale gains stripped out.

FFO reconstruction: undo the accounting fictions to find the cash.

One refinement deeper sits AFFO (adjusted FFO): FFO minus the recurring capital spending that keeps buildings leasable — new roofs, lobby renovations, leasing commissions. AFFO is the closest thing to the true cash available for dividends. The core REIT valuation stat is therefore P/AFFO (or P/FFO), used exactly as you'd use P/E on a normal stock: compare it to the REIT's own history and its sector peers. Quality apartment and industrial REITs have typically traded at 18–25× FFO; struggling office REITs at 6–10× — the market's way of saying it doesn't believe the F in their FFO.

The dividend: test it, don't trust it

REIT investors buy dividends, so test the dividend's plumbing. Compute the AFFO payout ratio: dividends ÷ AFFO. Below ~80%, the dividend has a cushion and room to grow. At 95%+, one bad quarter forces a cut. And a screaming 11% yield is almost never a gift — it's the market pricing in a cut before management admits it. The 2020 lesson: mall and hotel REITs yielding 10%+ in February slashed or suspended dividends by May. Yield is an output of price; distress is usually the input.

Dividend Stress Test

REIT pays $2.10/share, AFFO $2.35/share: payout 89% — functional, but no room for a recession. Its competitor pays $1.80 on $2.60 AFFO: 69% payout, growing 6% a year. The second stock yields less today and is worth more — the first is renting you your own principal.

Balance sheet and NAV

REITs live on borrowed money, so two more checks. Net debt / EBITDA: under 5.5× is investment-grade territory; over 7× means the dividend competes with lenders for every dollar. Also check the debt maturity wall — a REIT rolling 40% of its debt in a high-rate year gets repriced brutally. Then NAV — net asset value: what the buildings would fetch at market cap rates, minus debt. When a REIT trades far below NAV, you're buying buildings at a discount through the stock market — sometimes a genuine bargain (2009, 2020), sometimes a value trap where the market has correctly concluded the buildings' marks are stale (office, 2023). The discount is the beginning of the question, not the answer.

The Five-Minute REIT Checklist

Sector and its demand story → P/AFFO vs peers and own history → AFFO payout ratio under ~85% → net debt/EBITDA under ~6× with no near-term maturity wall → same-store NOI growth positive. Five numbers, five minutes, and you're ahead of nearly everyone quoting dividend yields at parties.

Sectors are the strategy

Most of a REIT's fate is its sector. Industrial and data centers ride e-commerce and AI compute demand; cell towers are utilities with contracts; apartments track wage growth and local supply; grocery-anchored retail proved durable while malls decayed; office is a falling knife with a narrative problem; healthcare REITs lease to operators whose margins are political footballs. Buy the sector thesis first, the specific REIT second — and if you don't want to choose, a broad REIT index fund holds them all, letting sector winners compound over the losers.
