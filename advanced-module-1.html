Futures Contracts: The Basics

A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a future date. Unlike options, both parties are obligated to fulfill the contract. Futures trade on regulated exchanges like CME with standardized contract sizes and settlement dates.

Key Concept

Futures prices converge to spot prices at expiration due to arbitrage. A futures contract 6 months out trades at a premium to today's price when interest rates are positive. This is called "contango." Understanding basis risk (difference between futures and spot) is critical for hedging.

Margin, Leverage, and Settlement

Futures require margin deposits (typically 5%-20% of contract value), enabling 5x-20x leverage. Daily settlement marks positions to market price. Losses immediately reduce account equity; gains flow in. Leverage amplifies both gains and losses—a 10% move in the underlying causes 50%-200% swings in margin account value.

Interest Rate Swaps

A swap is a bilateral agreement to exchange cash flows. In a fixed-for-floating interest rate swap, one party pays fixed interest while receiving floating (usually LIBOR+spread). Banks use swaps to convert loan types without refinancing. The notional amount is not exchanged—only the interest differentials.

Structured Products: Turning Risk into Returns

Structured products combine bonds with derivatives to create custom payoff profiles. A reverse convertible pays high coupons but converts to stock if it falls below a barrier. Autocallables return capital early if stock stays above a level. These products trade liquidity and complexity for yield.

Margin Trading and Leverage Risk

Margin allows borrowing against securities to increase position size. A 50% margin requirement means $1 can control $2 of stock. If stock falls 30%, the account is wiped out. Forced liquidation at the worst prices accelerates losses. Professional traders use leverage but with strict risk limits and stop losses.

Key Takeaways

  1. Futures enable 5x-20x leverage through margin, with daily settlement exposing you to rapid losses.
  2. Swaps exchange cash flows between parties to manage interest rate or currency risk without ownership transfer.
  3. Structured products add complexity for enhanced yield but create call risk and issuer credit exposure.
  4. Leverage is not free—margin interest, daily mark-to-market, and forced liquidation extract real costs.
  5. Derivatives magnify both gains and losses. A 10% move in the underlying becomes 50-200% in a margin account.
