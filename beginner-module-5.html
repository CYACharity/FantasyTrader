Trading Strategies

Practical approaches to buying and selling stocks, from long-term holding to systematic investing.

~8 min read
Sources: MIT 15.401, Rice University
Last updated Mar 2026

Why You Need a Strategy

Trading without a plan is gambling. Research from behavioral finance—a field pioneered by behavioral economists and extensively studied in MIT's 15.401 curriculum—shows that emotional decisions consistently underperform systematic approaches. When investors act on fear, greed, or recent news, they make suboptimal trades at the worst possible times: buying after prices have already risen and selling after prices have fallen.

A trading strategy is a documented plan that removes emotion from decision-making. It specifies entry criteria (when to buy), exit criteria (when to sell), position size (how much to buy), and maximum loss tolerance (what you can afford to lose). Written strategies are your most powerful tool for consistency and long-term success.

Key Insight

Behavioral finance research shows that investors who follow written plans outperform those who trade based on emotions or intuition. Your strategy becomes your rules—and rules protect you when your emotions are screaming to break them.

Buy and Hold

The simplest and historically most effective strategy for beginners is buy and hold: purchase quality stocks and hold them for years or decades. This is the approach championed by Warren Buffett and endorsed by academic research at MIT and Rice University. Buy and hold works because it aligns with how companies create value: through reinvested earnings, compounding, and long-term growth.

The beauty of buy and hold is that you avoid most trading costs and taxes (if held in a long-term account), and you benefit from compound growth. A stock that doubles every 10 years becomes 4x your investment in 20 years, 8x in 30 years. Time is your ally.

Compound Growth

If you invest $5,000 in a stock earning 10% annually (the historical S&P 500 average), you have $12,969 after 10 years. Keep it invested for 30 years, and it becomes $86,261. The power of compounding grows exponentially—do not interrupt it by trading in and out.

Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)

Dollar-cost averaging is a systematic approach where you invest a fixed amount at regular intervals—monthly, quarterly, or annually—regardless of the stock's price. This removes the pressure to time the market (which no one can do consistently) and has the added benefit of buying more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high.

DCA is particularly effective during volatile markets. Instead of worrying about whether now is the "right" time to invest, you commit to a schedule and execute it mechanically. Over time, your average cost per share tends to be lower than if you tried to pick a single "perfect" entry point.

Example — DCA in Action

Suppose you invest $1,000 quarterly in a stock:

Q1: Stock price $100 → You buy 10 shares. Q2: Stock price $80 → You buy 12.5 shares (more shares because price dropped). Q3: Stock price $120 → You buy 8.33 shares. Q4: Stock price $100 → You buy 10 shares.

Total invested: $4,000. Total shares: 40.83. Average cost per share: $97.95. If you had tried to "time it" perfectly and invested all $4,000 at Q1's $100 price, you would have 40 shares at $100 average cost. DCA slightly beat the "perfect" timing.

Setting Investment Goals

Different investment goals require different strategies. Your time horizon, risk tolerance, and return expectations should dictate your approach. The Rice University investment curriculum emphasizes that short-term and long-term goals are fundamentally different beasts.

Goal Type Time Horizon Typical Strategy Risk Tolerance
Short-Term (Savings) 0–2 years Cash, money market, stable bonds Very low—preservation matters
Medium-Term (Down Payment) 3–7 years Balanced mix of stocks & bonds Moderate—some volatility acceptable
Long-Term (Retirement) 10+ years Majority stocks with diversification High—market fluctuations are temporary

If you need the money in 2 years, do not invest heavily in stocks—the short time frame means you cannot wait out a market downturn. If you are investing for retirement 30 years away, stocks should dominate your portfolio because time smooths out volatility and you capture compound growth.

Creating a Trading Plan

A written trading plan should include five components:

1. Entry Criteria

When will you buy? Examples: when a stock's price-to-earnings ratio drops below 15, when it breaks above a key resistance level, or on a fixed schedule (DCA). Be specific.

2. Exit Criteria

When will you sell? Examples: when the price reaches your target (e.g., +50% gain), when fundamentals deteriorate, or after a set holding period. You should also define a stop-loss—a price at which you automatically exit to limit losses (e.g., if down 20%).

3. Position Size

How much of your portfolio will any single stock represent? Most professionals recommend no more than 3–5% per position for beginners. This prevents one bad trade from derailing your overall results.

4. Risk Management

What is the maximum loss you are willing to accept? If you have $100,000 to invest, are you willing to lose $5,000 on a single trade? Define this before you buy, not after losses start mounting.

5. Rebalancing Rules

As stocks appreciate (or depreciate), your portfolio allocation shifts. Your plan should specify when you rebalance—perhaps quarterly or annually—to stay aligned with your goals.

Write It Down

A trading plan only works if you actually write it and stick to it. Post it above your desk. Review it before each trade. The moment you break your rules is the moment your strategy becomes worthless.

Common Beginner Mistakes

Four costly errors derail most new investors:

Overtrading: Treating investing like a game. Frequent trading generates commissions, taxes, and emotional exhaustion. Research on brokerage accounts consistently finds the vast majority of frequent traders underperform simple buy-and-hold.

Chasing Hot Tips: Following social media hype, tips from friends, or financial journalists recommending "the next big stock." By the time you hear about it, smart money has already moved on. Stick to your criteria.

Panic Selling: Selling during downturns when emotions are highest. Market corrections (10–20% drops) are normal. If you sell every time prices fall, you crystallize losses and miss the recovery. Your strategy should keep you calm.

Lack of Diversification: Putting all your money into one stock or sector. If that stock fails or the sector falls out of favor, you suffer catastrophic losses. Diversify across industries, company sizes, and asset types.

Key Takeaways

  1. A written trading strategy removes emotion and increases consistency. Behavioral finance research confirms that systematic approaches beat emotional trading.
  2. Buy and hold with compound growth is the simplest and most reliable long-term strategy for most investors.
  3. Dollar-cost averaging removes market-timing pressure and often results in lower average purchase prices than trying to pick the "perfect" entry.
  4. Match your investment strategy to your time horizon: short-term goals need safety, long-term goals can tolerate volatility for higher returns.
  5. Your trading plan must include entry criteria, exit criteria, position size limits, risk management, and rebalancing rules—and you must write it down and follow it.
