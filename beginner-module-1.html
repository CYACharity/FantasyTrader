Introduction to Financial Markets

What markets are, why they exist, and how stocks fit into the larger financial system.

12 min read
Sources: MIT 15.401, Rice University
Last updated Mar 2026

If you have ever wondered where your money goes when you invest in a stock, or why prices on the news seem to move every second, this module will give you a grounded understanding of how financial markets actually work. Before learning to trade, it is important to understand the system you are trading within.

Financial markets exist to solve a fundamental economic problem: connecting people who have capital with people who need it. A startup founder needs money to build a product. A retiree needs their savings to generate income. Financial markets are the mechanism that brings these two parties together, and they do so on a massive scale every single day.

What Is a Financial Market?

A financial market is any marketplace where buyers and sellers participate in the trade of financial assets such as stocks, bonds, currencies, and derivatives. These markets facilitate the flow of money from those who save it to those who put it to productive use. In doing so, they perform several essential functions for the economy.

Professor Andrew Lo of MIT Sloan identifies this as the central role of finance: the study of how resources are allocated over time under conditions of uncertainty. Markets provide the infrastructure for that allocation. They give companies access to funding, give investors access to returns, and give both parties a transparent way to discover prices.

Key Concept

Financial markets serve three primary functions: price discovery (determining what an asset is worth based on supply and demand), liquidity (the ability to buy or sell quickly without significantly affecting the price), and risk transfer (allowing participants to shift risk to those more willing to bear it).

Real Assets vs. Financial Assets

One of the first distinctions you will encounter in formal finance education is the difference between real assets and financial assets. This distinction comes from the introductory material of Rice University's Global Financial Markets and Instruments course, and it is a useful framework for understanding what you are actually buying when you invest.

Real assets are tangible things that produce goods and services: factories, land, machinery, intellectual property, and human capital. These are the productive assets of the economy. Financial assets, on the other hand, are claims on those real assets. A stock certificate does not give you a factory, but it gives you a legal right to a share of the profits that factory generates.

This distinction matters because when you buy a share of Apple stock, you are not buying an iPhone or an Apple Store. You are buying a financial claim that entitles you to a fraction of the profits Apple earns from those real assets. The value of that claim depends on how well Apple uses its real assets to make money.

The Three Major Types of Financial Instruments

Both the MIT and Rice curricula introduce three core categories of financial instruments early on. Each one represents a different kind of relationship between the investor and the entity that issued the instrument.

Equity (Stocks)

When you buy equity in a company, you become a part-owner. You have a residual claim on the company's earnings, which means you get paid after the company meets all its other obligations (like paying employees, suppliers, and lenders). The upside is theoretically unlimited: if the company does well, the stock price rises and you profit. The downside is that you can lose your entire investment if the company fails. Stockholders are last in line during a bankruptcy.

Fixed Income (Bonds)

A bond is essentially a loan. When you buy a bond, you are lending money to a company or government in exchange for periodic interest payments and the return of your original investment at maturity. Bonds are generally considered less risky than stocks because the interest payments are contractually guaranteed (unless the issuer defaults). However, the returns are also lower.

Derivatives

Derivatives are financial contracts whose value is derived from the price of another asset (like a stock or a commodity). Options and futures are the most common types. These are more complex instruments that are typically used for hedging risk or speculation, and we will cover them in later modules.

Why Stock Prices Move

This is one of the most intuitive but also most misunderstood aspects of financial markets. At the most basic level, stock prices are determined by supply and demand. When more people want to buy a stock than sell it, the price goes up. When more want to sell than buy, the price goes down. But what drives that demand?

MIT's Finance Theory I frames this through what Professor Lo calls one of the six principles of finance: financial market prices shift to equalize supply and demand. Prices are not arbitrary numbers. They represent the market's collective judgment about the future earnings potential of a company, adjusted for risk and the time value of money.

Example — How News Affects Price

Suppose a pharmaceutical company announces that its new drug received FDA approval. Investors immediately recognize that the company will generate billions in new revenue. Demand for the stock surges as buyers rush in, and the price rises sharply, sometimes within seconds.

Conversely, if a company reports earnings that fall short of analysts' expectations, investors may sell their shares, increasing supply relative to demand. The price drops. This is price discovery in action: the market is constantly processing new information and adjusting prices to reflect it.

Risk and Return: The Fundamental Tradeoff

Perhaps the most important concept in all of finance is the relationship between risk and return. Both the MIT and Rice courses emphasize this from the very beginning because it governs almost every investment decision you will ever make.

The principle is straightforward: higher potential returns come with higher risk. If you put your money in a U.S. Treasury bond, your return is nearly guaranteed, but it will be low. If you invest in a small startup's stock, you might earn extraordinary returns, but you also might lose everything. There is no way to earn high returns without accepting higher risk. MIT's framework calls this the "no free lunch" principle.

How Stocks Are Traded

Understanding where and how trading happens is essential before you start making trades yourself. Stocks are bought and sold on exchanges, organized marketplaces with rules and technology that match buyers with sellers.

The two largest stock exchanges in the world are the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and the NASDAQ. When you place an order to buy a share of Tesla, your brokerage routes that order to an exchange where it is matched with someone willing to sell at your price. This entire process takes milliseconds.

Rice University's course also introduces the concept of market orders (buy or sell immediately at the best available price) versus limit orders (buy or sell only at a specific price or better). Understanding this distinction is practical knowledge you will use every time you make a trade in FantasyTrader's practice environment.

Remember

You do not need to understand every technical detail before you start practicing. The purpose of this module is to give you enough context so that when you buy your first stock in the practice portfolio, you understand what you are buying, where it is being traded, and why the price changes.

Key Takeaways

  1. Financial markets connect savers with borrowers and perform price discovery, provide liquidity, and enable risk transfer.
  2. Financial assets (stocks, bonds) are claims on real assets (factories, equipment). When you buy a stock, you own a claim on the company's future profits.
  3. Stocks offer higher potential returns than bonds, but come with greater risk. Bondholders are paid before stockholders.
  4. Stock prices are determined by supply and demand, which in turn are driven by new information about a company's expected future earnings.
  5. Higher expected returns always come with higher risk. This is the most fundamental principle in investing.
