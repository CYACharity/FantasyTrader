Stablecoins, Ethereum & Crypto's Plumbing

Alternative Investments Intermediate 4 min read

The dollar that lives on a blockchain

Bitcoin gets the headlines, but the workhorses of crypto are stablecoins, tokens engineered to always be worth $1. Tether (USDT) and USD Coin (USDC) settle more daily volume than Bitcoin and Ethereum combined, because they solve crypto's most practical problem: traders and businesses want blockchain speed without betting their working capital on volatility. A stablecoin is how you "hold dollars" inside a system that never closes, settles in minutes worldwide, and doesn't care what country you're in.

The engineering question is what backs the peg. Fiat-collateralized coins (USDC, USDT) claim a real dollar or Treasury bill in reserve per token, their risk is custodial honesty, which is why attestations and audits matter and why Tether's opacity has been a decade-long controversy. Crypto-collateralized coins (DAI) lock $1.50+ of volatile crypto per $1 issued, absorbing swings with over-collateralization. Algorithmic coins backed by nothing but code and confidence have a graveyard: TerraUSD's $40B death spiral in May 2022 proved that a peg maintained by circular incentives fails exactly when everyone leaves at once, a digital bank run with no deposit insurance.

The Depeg Test

March 2023: Silicon Valley Bank fails, and $3.3B of USDC's reserves are trapped inside. USDC trades down to $0.87 within hours, the peg IS the reserve, and the market repriced the doubt instantly. When the deposits were guaranteed that Monday, USDC snapped back to $1.00. Lesson: a stablecoin is exactly as stable as the thing backing it, discoverable only under stress.

Ethereum: the world computer

If Bitcoin is a calculator that does one thing perfectly, move value without a middleman — Ethereum is a computer anyone can program. Its smart contracts are programs that hold and move money according to their code: escrow without an escrow agent, lending pools without a bank, exchanges without a broker. This is DeFi, decentralized finance, and at its 2021 peak it held over $180B of deposits. The revolutionary claim isn't that DeFi is safer than banks (it isn't); it's that financial infrastructure became software anyone can build on, fork, and audit.

ETH the asset works differently from BTC. Ether is the fuel: every transaction burns a fee (gas), and since 2022's "Merge," Ethereum runs on proof-of-stake, holders lock ETH to validate blocks and earn ~3-4% yield, and heavy network usage burns more ETH than is issued. So ETH behaves less like digital gold and more like a tech-platform commodity: its value is a bet on the network's usage. The bear case is real too: competitors (Solana and friends) are faster and cheaper, fees push activity to "layer 2" networks that capture value ambiguously, and the SEC has never quite decided what ETH legally is.

Where the yield comes from (and when it's fake)

Crypto platforms advertise yields that make banks look silly. 4%, 8%, once 20%. The only honest question: where does the yield come from? Staking yield comes from real network issuance and fees, sustainable, modest. Lending yield comes from real borrowers paying real interest, sustainable if collateralized, as on-chain money markets like Aave mostly are. And then there was "yield" that came from new deposits paying old depositors. Celsius, BlockFi, Anchor's 20%, which is a Ponzi with a login page. All three imploded within 18 months. If you cannot trace the yield to a payer with a reason to pay, you are the yield.

The Plumbing Test

Before touching any crypto product, answer three questions in writing: What backs it? Who holds the keys? Where does the yield come from? Every crypto disaster of the last decade. Mt. Gox, Terra, Celsius, FTX, failed at least one of these questions in public, in advance, for anyone who looked.

Why this layer matters to an investor

You may never open a DeFi app, but this plumbing now touches traditional finance: BlackRock runs a tokenized Treasury fund on Ethereum, Visa settles some transactions in USDC, and stablecoin issuers are collectively a top-20 holder of U.S. Treasuries. The investable takeaways: stablecoins are a cash-management tool, not an investment (their upside is capped at $1 by design); ETH is a higher-beta, cash-flow-adjacent bet on blockchain usage that belongs, if anywhere, in the same small, speculative sleeve as BTC; and any yield above Treasury rates is compensation for a risk you must be able to name.
