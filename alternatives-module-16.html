Private Equity & Venture: Fund Mechanics

Alternative Investments Advanced 6 min read

The machine behind the logos

Module 15 gave you the waterfall. This module bolts it onto the two fund types that dominate private markets — buyout (PE) and venture (VC) — and gives you the numbers professionals actually argue about: capital calls, the J-curve, DPI versus IRR, and the anatomy of a leveraged buyout. The vocabulary shift matters: in funds, the pref is called the hurdle (typically 8%), the promote is called carried interest (typically 20%), and the management fee is 1.5–2% — on committed capital during the first ~5 years, then on invested capital. "2 and 20" is the same waterfall wearing a suit.

A fund is a 10-year contract. Years 1–5: the investment period, when the GP calls your committed capital in chunks as deals close — you wire money on two weeks' notice or suffer punitive dilution. Years 4–10: harvesting, when companies are sold and cash flows back. You cannot redeem. The only exit is selling your LP stake at a discount on the secondary market.

The J-curve and the three report cards

Fees are charged from day one on committed capital while investments are still marked near cost — so early returns look terrible even in funds that finish great.

Because of the J-curve, judging a young fund by IRR is like judging a marathon at mile two. LPs read three numbers together. DPI (distributions ÷ paid-in): cash actually returned — the only number that buys groceries. TVPI (distributions + remaining value ÷ paid-in): total scoreboard including unrealized marks the GP itself assigns. IRR: time-weighted, and gameable — call capital late, use subscription credit lines, return one early win, and a mediocre fund prints a dazzling early IRR. The seasoned-LP heuristic: trust DPI, respect TVPI, interrogate IRR. A 2015-vintage fund at 2.1× TVPI but 0.4× DPI in 2025 hasn't proven anything yet except valuation confidence.

IRR Games

Fund A calls $100 on day one, returns $200 in year 8: IRR ≈ 9%, DPI 2.0×. Fund B uses a credit line, calls your $100 only in year 3, returns $200 in year 8: same cash, same multiple — IRR ≈ 15%. Fund B's marketing deck wins; your bank account can't tell the difference. Subscription lines exist mostly to manufacture this exact optical upgrade.

Anatomy of a leveraged buyout

A buyout fund's craft is buying whole companies with borrowed money and selling them better — or at least more levered. Returns decompose into exactly three sources: EBITDA growth (operational improvement), multiple expansion (selling at a higher multiple than you paid — a market gift, not skill), and deleveraging (using the company's own cash flow to repay its acquisition debt, transferring value from the debt column to the equity column every quarter).

The standard LBO: 60% debt at purchase, cash flow pays down debt, equity captures the difference at exit.

The dark side is symmetrical. That $600M of debt belongs to the company, not the fund. If EBITDA falls 20%, interest coverage breaks, and the equity is worth little — while the fund's other portfolio companies are untouched (each deal is its own bankruptcy-remote box). Critics note this asymmetry: the GP earns fees on all deals and carry on winners, while creditors, employees and pensions absorb the losers. When you evaluate listed PE firms (Blackstone, KKR, Apollo), you are buying the fee stream and carry stream — a leveraged bet on their fundraising, not on any single deal.

Venture: the power law, quantified

Venture is the opposite construction: no debt, no control, 20–40 tiny positions, and a return distribution so skewed that the average outcome is meaningless. Correlation Ventures' dataset across thousands of financings: roughly 65% of deals return less than 1×, and about 4% return more than 10× — and that 4% produces the majority of all venture profit in existence. A fund needs one 30–100× company per fund; everything else is statistical noise. This is why VCs pass on solid businesses with obvious $50M outcomes: a guaranteed 3× on one position cannot carry a fund built for power-law hunting.

For your own portfolio the implication is sharp: angel checks and equity crowdfunding are lottery tickets whose expected value depends entirely on access to the exceptional 4% — which, definitionally, you and I mostly don't have. The honest retail routes into ventures' economics are listed managers, and accepting the illiquidity of interval funds that hold late-stage private names — both with fee stacks you now know how to read.

Reading a PPM in ten minutes

Fund documents run 200 pages; the economics live in five clauses. Fee basis: 2% on committed vs invested capital differs by hundreds of thousands per $10M over a fund's life. Hurdle and catch-up: 8% with 100% catch-up is standard; no hurdle is aggressive. Waterfall type: European or deal-by-deal (module 15 — and everything there applies). Fee offsets: portfolio-company "monitoring fees" should offset the management fee 100%; anything less means the GP bills its own portfolio and keeps the change. Key-person and no-fault-divorce clauses: what happens when the star leaves. Skip the market-opportunity section entirely; nobody has ever written a bearish one.

The Only Question

Every private-fund decision compresses to: is the expected illiquidity premium — historically ~2-4% annually over public equivalents, before dispersion — worth locking your money in a structure where the manager controls the marks, the timing, and the fees, for a decade? For top-quartile access, often yes. For the funds that actually accept $25k checks, run the fee math first.
