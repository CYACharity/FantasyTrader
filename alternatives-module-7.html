Rental Property Math, All the Way Down

Alternative Investments Intermediate 4 min read

From daydream to spreadsheet

The beginner module gave you the half-expense rule of thumb. This module replaces thumbs with the four numbers professional rental investors actually compute before making an offer: NOI, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, and DSCR. Learn these and no listing agent's "great investment!" will ever move you again — only the arithmetic will.

Start with NOI — net operating income: all rental income, minus vacancy loss, minus operating expenses (taxes, insurance, repairs, management, utilities you cover). Crucially, the mortgage is NOT in NOI. That's deliberate: NOI measures what the property earns regardless of how you financed it, which is what makes properties comparable to each other.

NOI, Line by Line

Duplex, both units at $1,400/mo: gross $33,600. Vacancy at 5%: −$1,680. Taxes $3,900, insurance $1,800, repairs $2,400, management (8%) $2,554, water/trash $1,200. NOI = 33,600 − 1,680 − 11,854 = $20,066. The mortgage never appeared — on purpose.

Cap rate: the property's yield

Divide NOI by price and you get the cap rate — the property's unlevered yield. Our duplex at a $290,000 asking price: 20,066 ÷ 290,000 = 6.9%. That single number lets you compare a duplex in Cleveland to a fourplex in Tampa to a Treasury bond. Cap rates move inversely with prices and reflect risk: a 4.5% cap on a new building in Austin means the market considers it safe and priced for growth; a 9% cap in a shrinking rust-belt town is the market demanding a bribe to accept the risk. Neither is "better" — they're different bets, priced accordingly. A cap rate far above its neighborhood is not a bargain sign; it's a "look closer, something is wrong" sign.

Cash-on-cash: YOUR yield

Cap rate ignores financing; cash-on-cash is where the mortgage returns. Take actual annual cash flow after debt service, divide by actual cash invested (down payment + closing costs + upfront repairs). Duplex with 25% down ($72,500) plus $7,500 closing/repairs = $80,000 in. Mortgage on $217,500 at 6.8%/30yr ≈ $17,016/yr. Cash flow = 20,066 − 17,016 = $3,050. Cash-on-cash = 3,050 ÷ 80,000 = 3.8%. Notice what leverage did: the property yields 6.9%, but after paying the bank, your cash earns 3.8% in year one. Leverage below the loan rate is a headwind — this deal only works if rents grow or the price appreciates.

The full return picture: cash flow is just one of four engines, and often the smallest one early on.

The four-engine view rescues (or condemns) marginal deals. Our duplex: $3,050 cash flow + roughly $2,300 of year-one principal paid by tenants + appreciation at 3% on the full $290,000 ($8,700, levered on your $80k) + tax depreciation sheltering perhaps $1,000 of taxes. Total ≈ $15,000 on $80,000 — about 19% — if appreciation cooperates, and about 8% if it doesn't. Every rental argument between investors is really an argument over how much weight the appreciation engine deserves.

DSCR and the safety margin

Lenders check one more ratio: DSCR — NOI ÷ annual mortgage payments. Ours: 20,066 ÷ 17,016 = 1.18×. Most investment-property lenders demand 1.20–1.25×, so this deal sits on the approval boundary; a slightly bigger down payment fixes it. DSCR is also YOUR margin of safety: at 1.18×, rents can fall about 15% before the property stops covering its own mortgage and starts eating your salary. Deals bought at 1.0× DSCR in 2021's low-rate euphoria became forced sellers by 2023. Underwrite so a bad year is an annoyance, not a foreclosure.

The Offer Discipline

Professionals run this math backwards: decide the cash-on-cash you require (say 6% minimum with honest expenses), then solve for the price that produces it, and offer THAT — regardless of asking price. The listing price is the seller's dream; your spreadsheet is your offer.
