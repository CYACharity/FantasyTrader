International Investing & Currency Risk

Long-Term Investing Advanced 4 min read

The home-bias tax

U.S. investors hold ~80% of their equities at home while the U.S. is ~60% of world market cap, and every country does the same (Swedes overweight Sweden, Japanese overweight Japan), which mathematically cannot all be right. The case for going global isn't that foreign markets are "better"; it's regime insurance. The 2000s: S&P 500 total return NEGATIVE for the decade while emerging markets more than doubled. The 2010s: the exact reverse. Nobody rang a bell at either turn. Valuation gaps at extremes (foreign markets have recently traded at 12-14× earnings vs 20-22× for the U.S.) don't time anything, but they set the odds for the following decade.

The counterargument deserves honesty too: S&P 500 companies already earn ~40% of revenue abroad, correlations between developed markets have risen to 0.8+, and the U.S. structurally overweights the sectors that compound (software) while Europe overweights the ones that don't (banks, energy). Both cases are real. The resolution is boring and correct: a fixed global weight, whether 20% or market-weight, held through the regimes, rather than a rotating opinion.

Currency: the return you didn't order

One index, three returns: local, unhedged, and hedged. Currency is a separate position you hold whether or not you meant to.

Buy a Japanese fund and you own two things: Japanese stocks AND the yen. The math is multiplicative: (1 + local return) × (1 + currency return) − 1. Nikkei +22% while the yen falls 13%: your dollar return is 1.22 × 0.87 − 1 ≈ +6%… before the hedged version returned ~+14% net of hedge costs. Over months, currency can dominate; over decades, developed-market currencies have roughly washed out. Hence the professional convention: hedge bonds, don't hedge stocks, currency swings overwhelm bond yields but add tolerable noise to equity returns. And hedging isn't free: the cost is the interest-rate differential (hedging yen exposure while U.S. rates exceed Japan's by 4-5% costs 4-5% annually, sometimes the hedge IS the position).

Emerging Market Full Math

An EM fund's local index gains 18%. The currency basket drops 9% against the dollar (EM currencies fall precisely when EM risk rises, correlation, not accident). Fund fee 0.7%. Your return: 1.18 × 0.91 − 1 − 0.007 ≈ +6.7%. Not the 18% on TV. EM investing without currency awareness is ordering a meal without seeing the right column of the menu.

What's actually different out there

Developed ex-U.S. (Europe, Japan, Australia): same rule of law, different sector DNA, more dividends, more cyclicals, structurally cheaper, and prone to decade-long value runs when the dollar weakens. Emerging markets: a label containing incompatible things. Taiwan's chip monopoly, India's demographic compounding, China's state-directed markets where the government can delete an industry's profits overnight (education stocks, 2021: −90% by decree), plus commodity states levered to China's cycle. The EM risks worth naming: capital controls (Russia 2022: foreign holdings marked to zero), VIE structures (many Chinese ADRs are contracts with shell companies, not ownership), and governance where minority shareholders eat last. Diversification across EM countries matters MORE than within them.

Implementation Without Regret

A defensible structure: total international index at 20-40% of equities (or accept explicit home bias and write down why), hedged developed-market bonds if you hold foreign bonds at all, EM inside the index weight rather than as a side bet, and rebalancing bands that force buying whichever hemisphere just had its lost decade. What doesn't work: rotating between "US is unstoppable" (2021) and "international is cheap" (after it ran), the flows data shows retail does exactly this, buying each regime at its end.
