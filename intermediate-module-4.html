Correlation & Diversification Math

Correlation measures how two assets move together. Think of it like a dancefloor: some stocks dance together (high positive correlation), some move independently (correlation near zero), and some dance opposite (negative correlation). A +1 correlation means they're locked in sync (both gain or lose together). A -1 correlation means they're opposites (one goes up, the other goes down). The magic of diversification is combining assets with low or negative correlation—they don't move together, so their wobbles cancel out.

Key Insight

Diversification is not about making you richer. It's about making the ride smoother. By combining uncorrelated assets, your portfolio zigzags less on the way to the same overall returns. Less stress, fewer sleepless nights—that's the real value.

Example: You own 100% stocks—your portfolio is up 20% this year but dropped 15% in 2022. Very volatile. Now you diversify: 60% stocks, 40% bonds. Bonds have low correlation with stocks. Bonds rose in 2022 while stocks fell, cushioning the blow. Your portfolio only dropped 8%. Same long-term returns, but less dramatic swings.

Hedging Strategies

A hedge is like insurance on your portfolio. You pay a small price today to protect against a big loss tomorrow. The simplest hedge is owning bonds—when stocks crash, bonds usually rise, offsetting losses. You can also hedge with put options or inverse ETFs, but these are more active (and riskier) plays.

Put Options as Insurance

Buy a put option on your stock holdings as downside insurance. If the market crashes 20%, the put gains value and offsets some of those losses. Think of it like buying an umbrella for a rainy day—you pay a small premium ($3-5 per share) for protection. If the rain never comes, you lose the umbrella cost, but you sleep better.

Example: Your portfolio is worth $100,000, but you're worried about a market crash. You buy puts that protect you below $90,000. You pay $1,000 in insurance. If the market crashes and your portfolio falls to $80,000, your puts gain $10,000, leaving you with $90,000. The $1,000 insurance paid off.

Inverse ETFs

Inverse ETFs move opposite to the market. The PSQ goes up when the NASDAQ drops. These are quick-fix hedges for short-term panic, but avoid holding them long-term—they decay and bleed value due to how they're structured (daily rebalancing). Use them for temporary protection, not permanent holdings.

Tax-Loss Harvesting

Tax-loss harvesting is a legal trick to save money on taxes. When a stock drops and you have a loss on paper, sell it to lock in that loss for tax purposes. Then immediately buy a similar stock to stay invested. Use the loss to offset capital gains you've made elsewhere. It's like getting a tax-free refund from the IRS while staying invested in the market.

Example: You bought Tesla at $200, it's now at $150. You have a $5,000 loss. You sold Tesla and immediately bought Lucid (another EV company). You're still invested in electric vehicles, but now you have a $5,000 loss to offset gains from other winners. If you made a $5,000 gain selling Apple, these losses and gains net to zero on your tax bill. You just saved 20% (maybe $1,000) in taxes.

The Wash-Sale Rule

The IRS doesn't want you abusing this. If you sell at a loss, you can't buy the exact same security for 30 days (before or after the sale). But you can buy a similar one. Sold Tesla at a loss? Buy Lucid or NIO instead. You stay in the EV sector while harvesting the loss.

Rebalancing Strategies

Rebalancing Type Trigger Best For Pitfall
Calendar Fixed date (monthly, quarterly) Discipline, passive investors Ignores market conditions
Threshold Asset allocation drifts 5-10% Active traders, adjusting quickly More trading costs & taxes
Drift-Based When volatility spikes Risk-conscious investors Requires monitoring

Key Takeaways

  1. Low correlation between assets is the key to diversification. Combine uncorrelated assets to reduce volatility.
  2. Hedging reduces downside at the cost of upside. Common hedges: bonds, puts, inverse ETFs.
  3. Tax-loss harvesting lets you offset gains with losses, reducing tax bills without selling winning positions.
  4. Calendar rebalancing is disciplined and simple. Threshold rebalancing is more active but incurs more costs.
  5. Rebalancing forces you to "buy low, sell high" by restoring your target allocation.
