DCF in Practice: Valuing a Real Business

Long-Term Investing Advanced 4 min read

The theory in one sentence

A business is worth the cash it will hand its owners over its remaining life, discounted to today at a rate reflecting the risk of those cash flows. Everything else in valuation, every multiple, every price target, is a shortcut to this sentence. The discounted cash flow model does it explicitly, which is both its power (assumptions in daylight) and its danger (precision theater: a spreadsheet with 40 inputs still outputs an opinion).

The machine has four parts: forecast free cash flows for 5-10 explicit years; estimate a terminal value for everything after; discount both at the WACC (weighted average cost of capital); subtract net debt to get equity value, divide by shares. Each part is a place to lie to yourself, so we'll build one honestly.

A full worked model

Target: a software company, $1.0B revenue growing 12% tapering to 6% by year five, 24% FCF margins, $200M net cash, 100M shares. Step 1, explicit years. FCF: Yr1 $269M, Yr2 $298, Yr3 $327, Yr4 $353, Yr5 $375. Step 2. WACC. Cost of equity via CAPM: 4.3% risk-free + 1.1 beta × 5% equity premium ≈ 9.8%; no debt, so WACC ≈ 9.8%, call it 10%. Step 3, discount. Each year's FCF ÷ 1.10^t: 245 + 246 + 246 + 241 + 233 = $1,211M. Step 4, terminal value via perpetuity growth at 3%: TV = 375 × 1.03 ÷ (0.10 − 0.03) = $5,518M; discounted: 5,518 ÷ 1.10^5 = $3,426M. Enterprise value = 1,211 + 3,426 = $4,637M; plus $200M cash = $4,837M equity; ÷ 100M shares = $48.37.

Where the value sits in our model: the five forecast years contribute ~26%; the terminal value contributes ~74%.

Now the honesty audit. Terminal value is 74% of the answer, meaning the model's output is mostly two numbers we guessed: terminal growth (3%) and WACC (10%). Move terminal growth to 2%: price falls to ~$44. WACC to 9%: price jumps to ~$56. A ±1-point wiggle in either assumption moves the "intrinsic value" ±15%. Anyone quoting a DCF price target to the penny has confused the spreadsheet for the territory.

How professionals actually use it

Not as a price oracle, as a discipline engine, three ways. Sensitivity tables: value across a grid of WACC × terminal growth; if the current price sits outside the entire plausible grid (as 2021's story stocks did), that's signal. Reverse DCF: set the model equal to today's price and solve for the implied growth — "the market is pricing 18% FCF growth for a decade; has ANY company this size ever done that?" is a genuinely answerable question (very few; base rates from Mauboussin's work say double-digit decade-long growth is rare and mostly unpredicted). Scenario weighting: bear/base/bull models with probabilities, forcing you to write down what breaks the thesis. The output that matters isn't $48.37, it's the SHAPE: which assumptions the value is hostage to.

Reverse DCF on a Real Pattern

A stock trades at $95 when your base model says $48. Solving backwards: the price implies 20% growth for ten years at a 10% discount rate. Roughly 1-2% of companies historically sustain that. The stock isn't "wrong", it's making a claim. Your job is deciding whether this company is the 1 in 50, sized as if you might be wrong. That is a much better question than "is my target price right?"

The craft rules

DCF Hygiene

Terminal growth ≤ long-run GDP (~2-3%), anything higher assumes the company eventually becomes the economy. Reinvestment must fund the growth: growth without capex/R&D is spreadsheet magic. WACC below 8% for an equity needs justification. Terminal value above ~75% of total means your explicit period is too short or your story is all hockey-stick. And run the model on the SAME company twice a year, the drift in your own assumptions is the best measure of how much narrative has infected your process.

Final calibration: Buffett claims never to have written a DCF ("if the value doesn't scream at you, it isn't there"), while every bank intern builds five a week. Both are consistent: the model's purpose is to make mispricing OBVIOUS or expose that it isn't. If your edge requires the fourth decimal of WACC, you don't have an edge, you have a spreadsheet.
