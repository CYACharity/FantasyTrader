Risk Parity, Illiquidity & Portfolio Design

Alternative Investments Advanced 4 min read

The capstone question

Seventeen modules of instruments; this one is about assembly. The question that separates institutional portfolios from retail collections isn't "what should I buy?", it's "where does my risk actually come from, and am I being paid for each source of it?" Two ideas do the heavy lifting: risk-based (rather than dollar-based) allocation, and the honest pricing of illiquidity.

Dollars lie; volatility doesn't

The classic 60/40 portfolio is a stock portfolio with a bond garnish once you measure in risk units.

Quick math: stocks run ~16% annual volatility, bonds ~5%, with low correlation. In a 60/40, stocks contribute roughly 0.6×16 = 9.6 risk units against bonds' 0.4×5 = 2.0, about 90% of portfolio variance once correlation effects are counted. Your "diversified" portfolio rises and falls almost entirely with the equity market. Risk parity is the institutional response: allocate so each asset class contributes equal risk, which mechanically means more dollars in the low-volatility assets, and then, because a bond-heavy mix has a low expected return, leverage the whole thing back up to an equity-like risk level. Bridgewater's All Weather runs this at scale. The honest caveat: risk parity is structurally long bonds with leverage, so 2022, bonds and stocks down together as rates spiked, was its worst environment in decades. Every allocation framework has a regime that humbles it.

Risk Contribution Check

Portfolio: 70% stocks (16% vol), 30% commodities (20% vol), correlation 0.3. Naive risk shares: stocks 0.7×16 = 11.2, commodities 0.3×20 = 6.0, stocks are ~65% of risk despite commodities being the "risky" asset. Run this multiplication on your own portfolio; the answer is usually "you own one bet."

The illiquidity premium, real, and routinely overpaid for

Locking money up should earn extra return: the illiquidity premium, historically estimated at ~1–4% annually for private equity and private real estate over public equivalents. But three accounting artifacts inflate how it feels. Smoothing: private marks are appraisals, updated quarterly, anchored to last quarter, reported volatility is understated by half or more. Selection: indexes of private funds overweight survivors. The denominator effect: when public markets crash, private marks lag, so privates balloon as a percentage of the portfolio, institutions then can't rebalance into cheap public equities because they're "over-allocated" to privates at fictional marks. In 2022, U.S. pensions hit exactly this wall. If you allocate to interval funds or private deals, cap the sleeve (10–20%) based on money you demonstrably won't need for a decade, and treat the smooth statements as marketing, not measurement.

Volatility Laundering

A private real estate fund reporting 9% returns at 4% volatility owns the same buildings as a REIT index reporting 9% at 18% volatility. The buildings did not become calmer by being appraised quarterly. Cliff Asness's term for this is "volatility laundering", investors knowingly paying higher fees for the illusion of smoothness. If the smoothness is what you're buying, at least know that's the product.

An assembly protocol

A defensible alternative sleeve gets built in this order. One: fix total risk first, decide portfolio-level volatility (or maximum tolerable drawdown) before choosing ingredients. Two: classify every candidate by its job: return enhancers (PE, value-add real estate), diversifiers (managed futures, gold, market-neutral), inflation hedges (TIPS, commodities, infrastructure), income (credit, core real estate). Reject anything that can't name its job. Three: compute risk contributions, not dollar weights, using public-market proxies for anything privately marked. Four: stress the correlations, assume everything with equity beta goes to correlation 0.9 in a crash and see if the portfolio still survives. Five: write exit rules per position before entry: the fee change, drawdown, or thesis break that triggers a sale. Alternatives punish improvisation; the protocol is the edge.

Final note for the league and for life: every module in this pathway ends at the same three questions. What is the real risk, measured honestly? What is the total fee load, all layers included? And who controls the marks, the timing, and the exit? Answer those and you can walk into any alternative asset conversation. CRE capital stacks, waterfall promotes, fund PPMs, crypto custody, as the person in the room who can't be sold smoothness dressed as safety.
