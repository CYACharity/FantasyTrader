Reading Earnings Reports

Earnings reports come out quarterly, and traders obsess over them. The market doesn't care about the absolute number—it cares about the surprise. Did the company beat, miss, or meet expectations? Focus on three things: EPS (Earnings Per Share, which is profit divided by number of shares), revenue growth, and forward guidance (management's forecast for next quarter/year). Revenue is the foundation; EPS that beats only through accounting tricks is worthless.

Key Insight

The stock's reaction depends on surprise and guidance. Beat earnings by 10% but lower forward guidance? Stock drops. Miss earnings by 5% but raise guidance? Stock soars. Traders are betting on the future, not celebrating the past.

Example: Apple reports Q3 earnings. Analysts expected $1.20 EPS. Apple reports $1.25 EPS (beat!). But Apple lowers guidance to $1.15 for Q4 (weakness ahead). Stock drops 5% despite beating current quarter. The market looks forward, not backward.

P/E Ratio: Deeper Dive

P/E is the price tag on earnings. P/E = Stock Price / EPS. A P/E of 25 means investors are willing to pay $25 today for every $1 of profit the company makes per year. A low P/E can mean the stock is cheap (or it could mean the company is broken). A high P/E usually means investors expect fast growth. Always compare P/E to the company's sector average and its own history.

Example: Microsoft trades at a P/E of 35. The average tech stock trades at 25. Microsoft is expensive. But Microsoft grows at 15% per year, while the average tech stock grows at 5%. The higher P/E is justified. Conversely, GE trades at P/E of 12 while the market average is 18. GE is cheap, but it's growing slowly, which explains the low valuation.

Forward P/E vs Trailing P/E

  • Trailing P/E: Based on the past 12 months' actual earnings. It's always accurate but backward-looking. Good for mature, stable companies.
  • Forward P/E: Based on analyst estimates for the next 12 months. It's forward-looking but estimates are often wrong. Useful for growth stocks where earnings are accelerating.

DCF: Discounted Cash Flow (Simplified)

DCF is like asking: "How much is this company's future cash worth in today's dollars?" You project the cash the company will generate each year, then "discount" (reduce) each year's value because money today is worth more than money later. If you're confident the company will generate lots of cash, the DCF value is high. If the current stock price is lower, it's a bargain.

The discount rate is how much you demand as a return to wait for that money (typically 8-12% for stocks). If you run a DCF analysis and get a fair value of $100 per share, but the stock trades at $70, you found a 43% discount. That's potentially a buying opportunity.

Example: You project Coca-Cola will generate $1 billion in free cash each year for 10 years. Using a 10% discount rate, your DCF math says Coca-Cola is worth $20 billion. If Coca-Cola's market cap is only $15 billion, it's undervalued. But if it's trading at $30 billion, skip it—even Coca-Cola at 50% premium is expensive.

Comparable Company Analysis

Comparable company analysis (often called "comps") is the lazy investor's shortcut: Instead of projecting cash flows for years, find similar companies and see what multiples the market pays for them. If Netflix trades at 4x revenue and you're looking at a similar streaming company trading at 1x revenue, maybe it's undervalued. If Netflix trades at 4x revenue and two other competitors do too, 4x is probably fair.

Example: You want to value Amazon. You check similar e-commerce and cloud peers: Shopify, Alibaba, Microsoft. They all trade at around 8x revenue. Amazon also trades at 8x revenue. If Amazon dropped to 5x revenue, it looks cheap compared to peers—a potential buying opportunity.

Key Takeaways

  1. Earnings surprise (beating/missing estimates) drives stock reactions more than absolute earnings.
  2. P/E ratio is a quick valuation gauge. Compare to sector average and history for context.
  3. DCF is theoretically sound but depends heavily on discount rate and growth assumptions.
  4. Comparable company analysis is practical and market-based; useful for peer valuation.
  5. Smart investors use all three methods to triangulate fair value and margin of safety.
