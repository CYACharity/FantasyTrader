Real Estate Debt: How Lending Really Works

Alternative Investments Advanced 6 min read

The other side of the table

Most investors only ever meet real estate debt as borrowers. But debt is a place to invest, an engine that sets property prices, and, when it goes wrong, the mechanism by which buildings change hands involuntarily. Most commercial buildings are bought with 50–65% borrowed money, trillions of dollars of loans in aggregate. If you understand how lenders size, price and enforce those loans, you can read almost any deal, and spot the ones that were dead on arrival.

A lender's worldview is asymmetric. Their best case is getting their money back with interest; they never share the upside. So the entire craft of lending is measuring and pricing the downside. Three numbers do nearly all of that work: LTV, DSCR, and debt yield.

The three numbers that size every loan

Loan-to-value (LTV) is the blunt one: loan ÷ property value. At 65% LTV, the building must lose more than a third of its value before the lender is underwater. But LTV inherits every flaw of the appraisal, in bubbles, "value" inflates and LTV flatters. DSCR, debt service coverage ratio, is the operating test: NOI ÷ annual debt payments. A DSCR of 1.25× means the building earns 25% more than the mortgage costs; most stabilized lending demands 1.20–1.30×. Below 1.0×, the property doesn't cover its own mortgage and someone is feeding it cash monthly.

DSCR is the lender's margin of safety: how far income can fall before the building stops covering its mortgage.

Debt yield is the cynic's metric: NOI ÷ loan amount, no appraisal, no interest rate, no amortization schedule to game. A $2M NOI on a $20M loan is a 10% debt yield, meaning if the lender forecloses tomorrow, they own an asset yielding 10% on their basis. CMBS lenders typically refuse to go below 8–10% debt yield regardless of what the appraisal says. When you hear "the deal doesn't pencil," one of these three numbers said no.

Sizing a Loan

NOI $2.0M. Value $30M. Lender terms: max 65% LTV, min 1.25× DSCR (rate 6.5%, 30-yr amortization ≈ 7.58% annual constant), min 9.5% debt yield. LTV cap: $19.5M. DSCR cap: 2.0 ÷ 1.25 = $1.6M payment capacity ÷ 0.0758 = $21.1M. Debt-yield cap: 2.0 ÷ 0.095 = $21.1M. The loan is the LOWEST of the three: $19.5M. Lenders always take the minimum, borrowers always remember the maximum.

Fixed vs. floating, and the 2022 object lesson

Fixed-rate debt (banks, life insurers, CMBS) trades certainty for prepayment handcuffs, defeasance or yield-maintenance penalties that can cost millions if you sell early. Floating-rate debt (SOFR plus a spread) is the tool of value-add investors who want to renovate, refinance and exit in three years, flexible, and lethal when rates rise. Between early 2022 and late 2023 SOFR went from ~0% to over 5.3%. A $20M floating loan at SOFR+275 went from ~$560k of annual interest to ~$1.6M. Deals underwritten at 1.3× DSCR fell straight through 1.0×.

Why "cheap" floating debt bankrupted good buildings: the rate you start with is not the rate you finish with.

The partial antidote is an interest-rate cap, an insurance contract that reimburses the borrower when the index exceeds a strike. Lenders on floating deals require them. In 2021 a two-year 2% cap on $20M cost a few thousand dollars; by 2023 the same cap cost hundreds of thousands. When cap premiums spike, that itself is the market telling you what it fears.

Recourse, covenants, and the paperwork with teeth

Most CRE debt is non-recourse: the lender's only remedy is the building, not the borrower's other assets, with "bad-boy carve-outs" that make the guarantee personal if the borrower commits fraud, files strategic bankruptcy, or strips cash out. Construction loans are usually full recourse until the building leases up. Covenants are the tripwires along the way: minimum DSCR tests each quarter, cash sweeps that trap all property income in a lender-controlled account when coverage slips, reserve accounts for taxes and re-leasing costs. A borrower can be current on every payment and still be in default on a covenant, and the sweep means the equity stops receiving distributions the moment the building weakens. Equity investors who never read the loan documents discover this at the worst possible time.

When it breaks: the default cascade

Miss a payment or trip a covenant and the sequence is mechanical. First, penalty interest and a workout conversation. Then a loan sale, lenders often sell troubled notes at 70–90 cents rather than foreclose, which is how distressed-debt funds buy buildings "through the debt." Then foreclosure (judicial, slow, borrower-friendly states) or a deed-in-lieu (hand over the keys, skip the courtroom). The mezzanine lender's remedy is faster and stranger: because mezz is secured by the equity of the owning entity under UCC Article 9, a mezz lender can auction that pledge in about 45 days and simply become the new owner of the building's owner, inheriting the senior loan intact. In every scenario, note the constant: the common equity's options run out first.

The Lender's Paradox

Debt feels safer than equity because it is, deal by deal. But a portfolio of 10% mezz loans has no winners to offset its losers: your best case is 10%, your worst case is zero. Equity portfolios heal through their winners; credit portfolios only avoid wounds. That is why credit underwriting discipline matters more than equity underwriting discipline, not less.

Investing in the debt yourself

You can own this asset class from a brokerage account. Mortgage REITs (commercial ones like Starwood Property Trust or Blackstone Mortgage Trust) originate the loans above and pay out the interest as dividends, note their own leverage: they borrow short to lend long, which is why 2020 and 2023 hit them hard. CMBS, pools of commercial mortgages sliced into rated tranches, are the securitized version of the capital stack, from AAA slices yielding a little over Treasuries down to below-investment-grade slices that behave like equity. Private debt funds originate bridge and mezz loans at 9–13% for accredited investors, with real lockups. In every wrapper, the questions are the ones this module gave you: what LTV, what DSCR, what debt yield, what happens when the tenant leaves?
