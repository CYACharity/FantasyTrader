Growth vs. Value Investing

Long-Term Investing Intermediate 3 min read

Two tribes, one arithmetic

Value investing buys businesses for less than a conservative estimate of what they're worth — Graham's "margin of safety," Buffett's cigar butts upgraded to wonderful-companies-at-fair-prices. Growth investing pays up for businesses compounding fast enough that today's price looks silly in a decade — Fisher's scuttlebutt, the Nifty Fifty, the FAANG era. The caricature war between them is marketing; the arithmetic is shared. Both are attempts to buy future cash flows for less than they're worth. They differ on where mispricing hides: value says markets overreact to bad news; growth says markets systematically underrate how long great companies can compound.

The evidence keeps both tribes humble. Value beat growth for most of a century (the academic "value premium," roughly 2-4% annually in the data) — then growth demolished value for the 2010s as software ate the world and zero rates made distant profits cheap to hold. Then 2022 flipped it again: rising rates crushed growth multiples while boring cash-now businesses held. Neither streak was permanent; both were regime-dependent.

Where each style fails

Value's failure mode is the value trap: statistically cheap, actually dying. Sears at 8× earnings, newspapers at 5×, Blockbuster at 4× — each looked like a bargain against yesterday's earnings while tomorrow's evaporated. The tell: cheapness alone, with declining revenue, eroding returns on capital, and a "this time the turnaround is real" narrative. Growth's failure mode is multiple collapse: the business performs, the stock still dies. A company at 40× that becomes a fine 4%-growth business at 18× loses you half your money while executing decently. Growth investors don't usually lose on bad companies — they lose on right company, wrong decade of price.

Multiple Collapse in Numbers

Stock at $100 = 50× on $2 EPS. Over five years EPS doubles to $4 (+15%/yr — genuinely great). But growth normalizing pushes the multiple to 20×. New price: $80. The company executed beautifully; you lost 20%. Entry multiple is destiny.

The synthesis grown-ups reach

Most great records converge to the same place from opposite doors: quality at a reasonable price. Buffett moved from cigar butts to Coca-Cola; growth managers learned to insist on profitability and moats. The operational checklist: high and stable return on invested capital (the subject of the next module), revenue growth above GDP, a valuation defensible against its own history and rates, and honest accounting of which regime you're betting on. For everyone else, the humble answer is owning both — a total-market index IS the truce — and letting the styles fight inside your fund instead of inside your head.

Style Self-Diagnosis

When a holding drops 30%, what's your instinct? "Great, cheaper" marks value temperament; "what changed in the story?" marks growth temperament. Neither is wrong — but strategy must match temperament, because you'll live inside its drawdowns. Value endures years of looking stupid before being right; growth endures crashes of 50%+ in businesses that are fine. Pick the pain you can hold.
