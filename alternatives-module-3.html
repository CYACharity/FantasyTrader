Real Estate for Beginners: Rent, Own, or REIT

Alternative Investments Beginner 3 min read

The three doors into property

Real estate is the world's largest asset class, and you can touch it three ways: live in what you own, own what others rent, or buy shares of companies that own buildings for you. Each door has a completely different price of admission, workload, and risk — and none of them is automatically "better." The mistake beginners make is treating them as one thing called "real estate."

Your own home is shelter first and an investment second. It pays you rent you no longer owe a landlord, and it locks in your housing cost — but it also eats property taxes, insurance, and roughly 1% of its value in maintenance every year, and it's expensive to sell. Historically, U.S. home prices have grown only slightly faster than inflation. The wealth-building power of a home comes mostly from the forced savings of a mortgage payment, not from spectacular price growth.

Being the landlord

A rental property is a small business. The income is real: a $250,000 house renting for $1,900 a month grosses $22,800 a year. But a business has expenses — taxes, insurance, repairs, months with no tenant, a property manager if you don't want 2 a.m. phone calls. A useful starting rule: expect about half of gross rent to disappear into expenses over time, before the mortgage. What's left over after everything is your actual profit, and on many properties it's much smaller than the listing photos suggest.

First Pass on a Rental

Rent $1,900/mo = $22,800/yr. Assume half goes to expenses: $11,400 left. Mortgage payment $10,200/yr. True cash profit ≈ $1,200 a year — about $100 a month — plus loan paydown and any price growth. The deal isn't bad; it's just honest math versus a daydream.

REITs: property without keys

A REIT (real estate investment trust) is a company that owns income property — apartment complexes, warehouses, data centers, hospitals — and is legally required to pay out at least 90% of its taxable income as dividends. Buy one share and you own a sliver of hundreds of buildings, managed by professionals, sellable in one click. The trade-off: REIT prices swing daily with the stock market, so you feel every mood swing you'd never notice in a house. For most people starting out, a broad REIT index fund is the simplest honest exposure to real estate that exists.

The Takeaway

All three doors work. A home builds savings slowly, a rental is a part-time job with leverage, and a REIT is real estate with a stock's convenience and a stock's volatility. Pick based on how much time, cash, and stomach you actually have — the intermediate modules ahead do the deeper math on doors two and three.
