Valuation: What a Company Is Worth

Long-Term Investing Intermediate 3 min read

Price is a vote; value is a weighing

A stock at $400 isn't "expensive" and a stock at $4 isn't "cheap" — price alone means nothing until divided by something the business produces. Valuation is the discipline of doing that division honestly. The workhorse is P/E: price per dollar of annual earnings. At P/E 25, you pay $25 for $1 of yearly profit — a 4% "earnings yield." The S&P 500's long-run average sits near 16–17; sustained readings far above that have historically preceded thinner decade-long returns. But P/E is only a starting point, because its denominator — earnings — is both manipulable and cyclical.

So professionals rotate lenses. P/S (price/sales) for unprofitable growers — harder to fake revenue than earnings. EV/EBITDA — enterprise value counts the debt too, so a company can't look cheap just by being leveraged; the standard tool for comparing capital-intensive businesses. P/FCF — price against actual free cash, the cynic's favorite. PEG (P/E ÷ growth rate) — a rough fairness meter for growth stocks: near 1 is reasonable, 3+ means priced for miracles.

Multiples are comparisons, not verdicts

Every multiple answers one question: compared to what? Compare to the company's own history (Microsoft at 35× vs its own decade average of 28× is a statement); to peers (a railroad at 22× among 15× railroads needs a reason); to the market and to interest rates — when 10-year Treasuries pay 5%, a 4% earnings yield is a bad deal, which is why rising rates crush high-multiple stocks hardest. A "cheap" P/E of 6 usually isn't a bargain — it's the market pricing decline (dying malls, secularly shrinking industries). The valuation question is never "is the number low?" but "is the market's implied forecast wrong?"

Two 6× Stocks

A tobacco firm at 6× earnings with flat volumes and monster dividends: the market forecasts slow decline; if decline is slower than priced, you win — the classic value case. A regional bank at 6× just before its loan book sours: the market forecast disaster and was right. Same multiple; opposite outcomes. The multiple was the question, not the answer.

Reverse-engineering expectations

The most powerful intermediate technique costs one minute: invert the multiple into a forecast. A company at 60× earnings must roughly double earnings twice in a decade just to grow into an average multiple at flat price. Ask: what growth does today's price REQUIRE? Then judge whether that requirement is plausible. Cisco in 2000 traded at 150× — the implied forecast was impossible, and the business went on to grow while the stock lost 80%. Nvidia at various points has carried 30×+ sales — sometimes the forecast was met, which is the humbling half of the lesson. Valuation doesn't predict; it prices the bar the company must clear.

The Habit

Before buying anything, write one sentence: "At this price, the market expects ___, and I believe ___ because ___." If you can't fill the blanks, you're not investing — you're subscribing to a vibe.
