Fundamental Analysis

How to evaluate a company's financial health, read its statements, and determine if a stock is worth buying.

~8 min read
Sources: MIT 15.401, Rice University
Last updated Mar 2026

Fundamental analysis is the practice of evaluating a company's intrinsic value — what it is genuinely worth — based on its financial statements and business operations. Unlike technical analysis, which focuses on price charts and trends, fundamental analysis asks a deeper question: Is this company's stock priced fairly given its actual earning power? MIT's 15.401 Finance Theory I framework teaches that security prices should reflect the present value of all future cash flows, adjusted for risk. Fundamental analysis is the disciplined method for estimating those cash flows.

What Is Fundamental Analysis?

At its core, fundamental analysis compares a company's stock price to measurable financial metrics: revenue, earnings, assets, liabilities, and cash flow. If a stock is trading at $50 but analysis suggests it should be worth $80, you may have found an undervalued opportunity. Conversely, if a $50 stock should theoretically be worth $30, it may be overpriced.

Key Concept

Fundamental analysis rests on the idea that stock prices eventually converge to a company's intrinsic value. In the short term, prices can diverge due to emotion, speculation, or incomplete information. But over time — months or years — fundamental value wins. Your job as an investor is to identify when price and value diverge, then position yourself to profit as they converge.

The Three Financial Statements

Every publicly traded company publishes three core financial statements each quarter and year. Together, they paint a complete picture of a company's health, profitability, and financial position.

Income Statement (Profit & Loss)

The income statement shows whether a company is profitable. It begins with revenue (total sales), subtracts all expenses (cost of goods sold, operating expenses, interest, taxes), and arrives at net income (profit or loss). The income statement answers: Did the company make money this period?

Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position)

The balance sheet is a snapshot of the company's financial position at a specific date. It lists all assets (what the company owns), all liabilities (what it owes), and shareholders' equity (what is left for owners). The fundamental equation is: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. The balance sheet answers: What does the company own, and how is it financed?

Cash Flow Statement

The cash flow statement tracks how much actual cash enters and leaves the company during a period. It reconciles net income (from the income statement) with actual cash movements, revealing how much free cash flow the company generates. This is crucial because a company can be profitable on paper but starved for cash if it is not collecting money from customers or is investing heavily in growth. The cash flow statement answers: Can the company actually pay its bills and fund growth?

Key Financial Ratios

Financial ratios distill a company's statements into a few crucial metrics that let you quickly assess value, profitability, leverage, and efficiency. Below are the most important ratios for fundamental investors.

Ratio Formula What It Measures Good Value
P/E Ratio Stock Price / Earnings Per Share How many dollars investors pay for each dollar of annual earnings 15–25 (depends on growth); S&P 500 avg ~20
P/B Ratio Stock Price / Book Value Per Share How many dollars investors pay for each dollar of assets (net of liabilities) 1.0–3.0; below 1.0 suggests undervalue
Debt-to-Equity Total Liabilities / Shareholders' Equity How much the company borrows relative to owner capital; leverage 0.5–2.0 (varies by industry); higher = more risk
ROE Net Income / Shareholders' Equity How efficiently the company uses shareholder capital to generate profit >15% is strong; <10% is weak
Example — Using the P/E Ratio

TechCorp stock trades at $100. Last year it earned $4 per share. The P/E ratio is 100 / 4 = 25. This means you pay $25 for every $1 of annual earnings. If the S&P 500 average P/E is 20, TechCorp is trading at a premium — suggesting either that investors expect faster growth, or that the stock is overpriced. You would compare this P/E to other tech companies, the market average, and the company's own historical range to judge if 25 is reasonable.

Growth vs. Value Investing

Two major schools of thought exist in fundamental analysis. Growth investors focus on companies with rapidly expanding revenues and earnings, accepting high P/E ratios in exchange for the prospect of capital appreciation. Value investors seek companies trading below their intrinsic value (low P/E, low P/B), betting that the market has misjudged them and that prices will eventually rise to reflect true worth.

The Difference

Growth: High P/E, high growth rates, reinvests profits, smaller dividends. Example: a biotech company with a $500M P/E but expected 30% annual earnings growth. Value: Low P/E, mature business, stable dividends, less growth. Example: a utility company with a $12 P/E and 2% growth but 4% dividend yield. Neither approach is objectively "better" — it depends on your risk tolerance and time horizon.

Industry Analysis: Context Matters

A critical mistake is evaluating a company's ratios in isolation. A P/E of 30 is alarming for a grocery chain (mature, stable, predictable) but reasonable for a software company (high growth potential, large market). Different industries have fundamentally different characteristics, margins, and growth rates. Always compare a company to its direct competitors and industry peers, not to the broad market.

Example — Industry Context

FoodMart, a supermarket chain, has a P/E of 22. The average grocery store P/E is 16. This suggests FoodMart is overpriced compared to peers. But TechSoft, a software company, has a P/E of 45 while its industry peers average 40. TechSoft looks more attractive. The same ratio means something different depending on industry norms, growth rates, and competitive positioning.

How to Use Fundamental Analysis in Practice

In FantasyTrader, you will apply these ideas by: (1) selecting a stock from the portfolio universe; (2) pulling up its latest financial statements (available through company fact sheets); (3) calculating or observing key ratios; (4) comparing those ratios to industry averages; and (5) deciding whether the stock is attractive relative to its price. This is not a guarantee of profit — the market is unpredictable — but it shifts the odds in your favor by keeping you disciplined and fact-based.

Key Takeaways

  1. Fundamental analysis evaluates a company's stock price against its intrinsic value, calculated from financial statements and future cash flow potential.
  2. The three core financial statements are the income statement (profitability), balance sheet (assets, liabilities, equity), and cash flow statement (actual cash movements).
  3. Key ratios like P/E, P/B, debt-to-equity, and ROE quickly reveal valuation, leverage, and efficiency. Ratios are most useful when compared to industry peers and historical averages.
  4. Growth investing prioritizes expansion and capital gains; value investing prioritizes undervaluation and margin of safety. Both are valid approaches.
  5. Always analyze a company within its industry context. Identical ratios mean different things for utilities, retailers, and tech companies.
