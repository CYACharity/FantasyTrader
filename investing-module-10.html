Moats: Durable Competitive Advantage

Long-Term Investing Intermediate 3 min read

Why great businesses stay great

Capitalism's default setting is erosion: any business earning fat profits attracts competitors until the profits are ordinary. A moat is whatever breaks that gravity — a structural reason competitors can't replicate the economics even when everyone can see them. Buffett's framing: he buys castles, but he pays for the moat. The word to underline is structural. Great products, hot growth and beloved CEOs are not moats; they're outcomes that moats protect.

The five moat sources — and the single metric that proves one exists.

Network effects: each user makes the product better for every other user (Visa's merchants↔cardholders loop) — the strongest moat and the most winner-take-all. Switching costs: leaving hurts more than staying (a bank replacing its core software risks its existence; your photo library keeps you in an ecosystem). Cost advantage: structurally lower unit costs via scale or process (Costco's buying power, TSMC's yield curve). Intangibles: brands that command price premiums (people pay double for the red can), patents, regulatory licenses. Efficient scale: markets that profitably support only one or two players (a second freight railroad between two cities would bankrupt both).

The proof is ROIC, not the story

Every annual report claims a moat; arithmetic settles it. Return on invested capital — after-tax operating profit ÷ (debt + equity capital employed) — measures what the business earns on the money inside it. No moat: competition drags ROIC toward the ~8-10% cost of capital. Real moat: ROIC holds at 15-30%+ for a decade THROUGH recessions and attack (Apple, Visa, Moody's run 25%+). The pattern to demand is high AND stable. One great year is a cycle; ten is a structure. Pair it with gross-margin stability: pricing power you can see in the P&L is the moat's fingerprint.

Moat Math

Company A reinvests $1B at 25% ROIC → $250M of new annual profit, compounding. Company B reinvests $1B at 7% (below its 9% cost of capital) → it destroyed $20M of value by growing. Growth is only good when ROIC beats the cost of the capital funding it. This single sentence separates compounders from empire-builders.

Moats die — watch the drawbridge

Kodak had the century's best brand moat; digital didn't care. Newspapers owned local-ad monopolies; Craigslist ate them in five years. Intel's process lead — a cost moat measured in physics — slipped to TSMC. Moat investing therefore isn't buy-and-forget; it's buy-and-verify: recheck ROIC trend, gross margin trend, and market share annually. The dangerous moment is when management stops widening the moat and starts harvesting it — cutting R&D, raising prices without adding value, financializing. That's the drawbridge creaking. The best operators (Amazon for two decades) do the opposite: torch reported profits to dig the moat deeper while Wall Street complains.

The Two-Question Filter

Before calling anything a moat: (1) If a competent rival spent $10B to take this market, WHY exactly would they fail? (2) Does a decade of ROIC comfortably above 15% confirm the story? A convincing answer to #1 without #2 is a narrative. #2 without #1 might be a cycle. Demand both.
