Commodities & Futures: Trading the Physical World

Alternative Investments Intermediate 4 min read

You can't buy a barrel (and you don't want to)

Commodities are the rawest asset class, oil, gas, copper, corn, coffee, and the only one you can't sensibly hold in a drawer. Nearly all commodity investing runs through futures: standardized contracts to buy or sell a fixed quantity at a fixed price on a fixed date. A trader who buys a December crude contract at $78 doesn't want 1,000 barrels in her driveway; she'll sell the contract before delivery, pocketing (or eating) the difference. Futures exist so farmers and airlines can lock prices, speculators are the liquidity that makes that insurance market work.

The first thing futures teach is embedded leverage: that one crude contract controls ~$78,000 of oil against maybe $6,000 of posted margin. 13:1 by default. A $4 move in oil is a ±$4,000 swing on your $6,000. This isn't a warning label; it's the mechanism. April 2020 made it unforgettable when expiring WTI futures printed −$37: holders with no storage literally paid others to take the oil. Physical settlement is not a metaphor.

The curve is the strategy

The futures curve, prices for the same barrel at different delivery dates, decides whether a commodity fund makes or bleeds money.

Since contracts expire, staying invested means rolling: selling this month's contract, buying the next. Whether that roll helps or hurts depends on the curve's shape. In contango (future prices higher than spot, the normal state, since storage costs money), every roll sells cheap and buys dear: a slow bleed called negative roll yield that can eat 5–15% a year. In backwardation (spot above futures, scarcity now), rolling actually pays you. This is why the USO oil ETF lost money over stretches when oil's spot price was flat or up: it owned the roll, not the barrel. Before buying ANY commodity fund, find out what it holds and what the curve looks like, the ticker's name is a vibe; the curve is the P&L.

Roll Math

Spot oil $80. Next-month future $81.60 (2% contango). A fund rolls monthly: even if spot ends the year at $80 exactly, twelve 2% rolls compound to roughly a −21% drag. The commodity was flat; the fund lost a fifth. Same fund in 2% backwardation: +27% with a flat commodity. The curve IS the return.

Energy and agriculture have different physics

Energy is geopolitics with a delivery schedule: OPEC decisions, wars, hurricanes in the Gulf, strategic reserve releases. It's also the inflation hedge that actually worked in 2022, energy was the only positive S&P 500 sector that year. Agriculture is weather with a balance sheet: droughts, freezes, export bans, and the annual rhythm of planting and harvest, which gives ag futures strong seasonal patterns. Industrial metals (copper, aluminum, lithium) are the economy's pulse, copper so reliably tracks global growth it has an economics degree ("Dr. Copper"), and the long-term electrification story runs straight through them. These groups often move independently, which is precisely their portfolio value.

Sane access routes

Ranked by sanity for a non-professional: broad commodity index funds (DBC, PDBC, BCI) spread across 10+ commodities with roll-optimization, the default choice, 5–10% of a portfolio at most. Producer equities (energy majors, miners, ag companies), leveraged to prices but they pay dividends and can hedge; you own a business, not a curve. Single-commodity ETFs, fine for gold (vaulted metal, no roll), treacherous for oil and gas (all curve). Direct futures, the professional tool, appropriate only with risk capital and a genuine understanding of margin calls. If the words "contango" and "margin" aren't comfortable yet, the list above is written in the right order.

The Role, Sized Honestly

Commodities earn no cash flow, so their long-run real return is roughly zero, their job is different: they spike when inflation surprises and when supply breaks, exactly when stocks and bonds struggle. That makes them seasoning, not the meal: 5–10%, rebalanced mechanically, judged by portfolio effect rather than standalone return.
