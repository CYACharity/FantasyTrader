Hedge Fund Strategies & Leverage Math

Alternative Investments Advanced 5 min read

What a hedge fund actually is

Strip the mystique: a hedge fund is a lightly regulated private pool that can short, use leverage and derivatives, and charge performance fees, the same "2 and 20" waterfall you already know, usually with a high-water mark (no incentive fee until past losses are recovered) and sometimes a hurdle. The strategies inside that wrapper differ so much that "hedge funds" as a category is as meaningless as "vehicles", a strategy label tells you more than the wrapper ever will.

This module walks the four big families with the actual math each one lives on: long/short equity and exposure accounting, merger arbitrage and spread math, global macro and asymmetric bets, and relative value, the strategy that works until it detonates.

Long/short and the exposure ledger

A long/short fund holds $130 long and $70 short per $100 of capital. Gross exposure = 130 + 70 = 200%, the total bet size, the leverage measure. Net exposure = 130 − 70 = 60%, the directional market bet. The pitch: make money on stock selection in both directions while dampening market swings. The math of the pitch: if longs rise 12% and shorts rise only 4% in an up market, the fund earns 1.3×12 − 0.7×4 = 12.8% before fees, better than the market with less net exposure. The catch: shorting has negative asymmetry (a short can lose more than 100%), borrow costs eat 1–8% annually on hard-to-borrow names, and crowded shorts can be squeezed 300% in a month, as GameStop taught several billion-dollar funds in 2021.

Merger arbitrage: pricing deal risk

The merger-arb trade in one picture: buy after the announcement, harvest the spread if the deal closes, absorb the gap down if it breaks.

Acquirer offers $40 cash for a target trading at $31. The stock jumps to $38.80, not $40. That $1.20 spread (3.1%) is the market's price for regulatory risk, financing risk, and time. Annualize it: 3.1% over an expected 5 months ≈ 7.6% annualized. The professional calculation is expected value: with a 94% close probability, EV = 0.94 × (+$1.20) + 0.06 × (−$7.80 fall back toward $31) = +$0.66 per share. Positive, but notice the shape: many small wins, occasional large losses. Arb desks earn a living precisely because that shape is uncomfortable; it is short-volatility income wearing an event-driven costume. In stock-for-stock deals the arb shorts the acquirer against the target, which is why acquirer shares dip on announcement day.

Global macro and relative value

Global macro bets on currencies, rates and commodities with big asymmetry hunting: Soros shorting sterling in 1992 (~$1B profit in a day when the UK left the ERM), Paulson's CDS position against subprime in 2007. The distinguishing discipline isn't prediction, it's structuring bets where being wrong costs 1 and being right pays 10. Relative value is the inverse temperament: harvest tiny mispricings between nearly identical instruments (on-the-run vs off-the-run Treasuries, convertible bonds vs their stock) and magnify them with 10–25× leverage. It produces years of smooth 1%-a-month returns, until a crisis forces every levered fund to unwind the same trades at once. LTCM ran ~25× leverage on exactly these trades in 1998, lost $4.6B in weeks, and required a Fed-brokered rescue. The lesson generalizes to every strategy in this module: leverage converts being eventually right into being immediately insolvent.

The Correlation Trap

Diversification across hedge funds is weaker than it looks: in stress, strategies that share a funding source (prime-broker leverage) or a position (crowded trades) converge to correlation ≈ 1. The 2007 "quant quake" saw dozens of market-neutral funds lose 10–30% in the same week, unwinding each other's positions. Ask any fund: who else holds your book, and what happens when they sell it?

The evidence, and the access question

Aggregate hedge fund returns have lagged a 60/40 portfolio for most of the past 15 years, net of fees, but the aggregate hides enormous dispersion: top-decile multi-strategy shops (Citadel, Millennium) have compounded in the high teens with low drawdowns, while the median fund earns beta and charges alpha prices. The retail translations, liquid-alt mutual funds, managed-futures ETFs, replication products, deliver watered-down versions of the factor exposures with daily liquidity. They can genuinely diversify a portfolio (managed futures were up ~20% in 2022 while stocks and bonds both fell), but treat them as diversifiers with modest expected returns, not as lottery tickets. Size them like insurance: 5–10%, funded from the bond side, judged over full cycles.

Fee Arithmetic

A fund returns 10% gross on your $100k. Fees: 2% management ($2k) + 20% of the 8% remaining ($1.6k) = $3.6k. You net 6.4%, the manager captured 36% of the gross return. At 6% gross, the manager's share rises to 46%. Performance fees are a call option on your capital that you sold for free.
