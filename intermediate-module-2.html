Options Basics

Master calls, puts, strike prices, and premium to unlock advanced trading strategies.

What is an Option?

An option is a bet on stock price movement without owning the stock itself. You pay a small fee upfront (called the premium) for the right—but not the obligation—to buy or sell 100 shares at a fixed price by a certain date. Options let you control big moves with small amounts of money. This leverage is powerful, but risky: you can lose your entire premium quickly if the bet goes wrong.

Key Concept

Every option has four parts: type (call = bet on a rise, put = bet on a fall), strike price (the fixed price level), expiration date (when the option expires), and premium (what you pay). Understanding these four pieces is the key to profiting with options.

Calls vs. Puts

Call Option

A call is a bet that a stock will rise. You pay a small premium for the right to buy at a fixed strike price. If the stock jumps above the strike, you can exercise (buy shares at the lower strike price) or sell the call contract itself for a profit. If the stock falls or stays flat, you lose the premium. Think of it like buying a lottery ticket that pays off if the stock goes up.

Example: You buy a Tesla $200 call with a 2-month expiration. You pay $5 premium. Tesla is trading at $195. If Tesla rises to $210, your call is worth $10+, and you double your money. If it falls to $185, your call expires worthless—you lose the $5.

Put Option

A put is insurance on a stock you own, or a bet that a stock will fall. You pay a premium for the right to sell at a fixed strike price. If the stock crashes below the strike, the put becomes valuable. If the stock rises, the put loses value and expires worthless. Puts are often used as "insurance"—you hope to never use them, but they protect you if the market tanks.

Example: You own 100 Netflix shares at $180 each. The stock has been volatile. You buy a $170 put for $3 (insurance). If Netflix crashes to $150, your put lets you sell at $170, limiting your loss. You paid $3 per share for peace of mind.

Strike Price & Expiration

The strike price is your fixed price level. It never changes—that's the whole point. Options closest to today's stock price cost less but move faster (more leverage). Expiration dates range from days to months. The closer expiration gets, the faster the option loses time value (a concept called theta decay). This is why long-term options tend to be better for beginners.

In-the-Money vs Out-of-the-Money

  • In-the-money (ITM): The option is already profitable if exercised today. A call with a $100 strike is ITM when the stock is at $110. A put with a $100 strike is ITM when the stock is at $90.
  • Out-of-the-money (OTM): The option would be unprofitable if exercised today. These are cheaper but riskier. You're betting on a bigger move.

Intrinsic Value vs Time Value

Every option premium has two parts. Intrinsic value is the profit you'd lock in right now if you exercised. Time value is what the market pays you for the chance that the stock will move further before expiration. As the expiration date approaches, time value evaporates fast. This is why selling short-dated options can be profitable—you're collecting time value that's about to disappear.

Example: You buy an Amazon $150 call when the stock is at $145. The call costs $8. Only $0 is intrinsic value (it's not ITM yet). The other $8 is time value. If Amazon stays at $145, that $8 time value disappears as expiration nears. But if Amazon jumps to $160, your $8 premium turns into $10+ intrinsic value, and you profit.

Basic Strategies

Covered Call

You own 100 shares and sell a call on top of them to collect premium. It's like renting out your shares for income. You get to keep the premium and any stock gains up to the strike price. The tradeoff: if the stock soars way above the strike, your shares get called away and you miss the upside. It's a conservative income strategy.

Example: You own 100 shares of Apple at $150. You sell a $160 call and collect $3 premium ($300 total). Apple is now "covered." If it stays below $160, you pocket the $300 and keep the shares. If it shoots to $170, your shares get called away at $160, and you miss the extra $10 per share of upside.

Protective Put

You own shares of a stock and buy a put option as downside insurance. It's like buying umbrella insurance for your portfolio. You pay a small premium now, and if the stock crashes, the put pays off and cushions your loss. If the stock rises, the put expires worthless, but you captured the gains. You paid for peace of mind.

Example: You own 100 Google shares bought at $130. You're worried about an earnings miss, so you buy a $125 put for $2 per share ($200 total). If Google crashes to $110 after earnings, your put is worth $15, offsetting your loss. The $2 insurance cost was worth the sleep you'll get.

Key Takeaways

  1. A call gives the right to buy; a put gives the right to sell.
  2. Strike price, expiration, and premium are the three pillars of options pricing.
  3. Intrinsic value is profit if exercised today; time value is what you pay for future optionality.
  4. A covered call caps upside in exchange for premium income.
  5. A protective put is insurance—you pay to protect downside.
