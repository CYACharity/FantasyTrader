Equity Waterfalls & the Promote

Alternative Investments Advanced 6 min read

Where the equity's money actually goes

The last two modules built the stack and priced the debt. This one answers the question every private real estate deal, private equity fund, and syndication ultimately turns on: when the property sells, who gets which dollar, in what order? The answer is a contract called the distribution waterfall, and if you invest in private deals without being able to model one, you are trusting arithmetic you've never seen.

Two roles first. The GP (general partner, sponsor) finds the deal, signs the loan, does the work, and typically puts in 5–10% of the equity. The LPs (limited partners) supply the rest and are passive. The waterfall exists to solve one problem: pay the LPs a fair base return on their capital first, then pay the GP a disproportionate share of the excess, the promote, as the prize for performance.

The four classic tiers

The standard American-style waterfall. Each tier is a bucket that must fill before the next receives anything.

Tier 1, return of capital. Every distributed dollar goes to LPs until they have their original investment back. Tier 2, preferred return. LPs then receive a compounding hurdle rate on their capital, usually 7–9% annually, for the time it was outstanding. The "pref" is not a guarantee, it's a priority. If the deal earns 5%, LPs get the 5% and the GP's promote is zero. Tier 3, the catch-up. Once LPs are whole plus pref, 100% (sometimes 50%) of the next dollars go to the GP until the GP has received its full percentage of the profits paid so far. Tier 4, the split. Everything above flows 80/20, LPs/GP. Deals frequently add IRR hurdles: above a 12% LP IRR the split becomes 70/30, above 15% perhaps 60/40. That escalating schedule is what sponsors mean when they say "the promote."

A full worked waterfall

Numbers make it honest. LPs invest $9M, the GP invests $1M (10% co-invest). Five years later the deal returns $18M of distributable cash. Pref is 8% simple for clarity: LP pref = 9M × 8% × 5 = $3.6M; the GP's co-invested $1M earns pref too: $0.4M.

Check the catch-up: after Tier 2, total profit distributed is $4.0M. For the GP to hold 20% of profits, it needs $1.0M against the LPs' $4.0M, hence Tier 3's size. Final tally: LPs put in $9M and got $15M − their share of GP economics... precisely: LPs receive $9M capital + $3.6M pref + $2.4M split = $15.0M, a 1.67× multiple. The GP turned $1M of capital into $1.0M back + $0.4M pref + $1.6M of promote = $3.0M. That asymmetry. 3.0× for the GP vs 1.67× for LPs on the same deal, is not a scam; it's the posted price of the GP's work. It becomes a scam only when the fees below make it a heads-I-win coin.

European vs. American, and the clawback

In a European (whole-fund) waterfall, the GP earns no promote until every LP dollar across the entire fund, plus pref, is returned. In an American (deal-by-deal) waterfall, the GP takes promote on each winning deal as it sells, even if later deals lose money. American waterfalls pay sponsors earlier, which is why sponsors prefer them and why LPs demand a clawback: an obligation to return over-distributed promote at the end, ideally backed by a personal guarantee and an escrow. A clawback without escrow against a GP entity with no assets is a polite fiction. When reviewing docs, this single clause tells you more about sponsor alignment than the pitch deck's entire "alignment" slide.

Fee Drag Is the Silent Tier

The waterfall splits profit, but fees are extracted before profit exists: 1–2% acquisition fee on the purchase price, 1–1.5% annual asset-management fee on equity, financing fees, disposition fees, property-management fees to an affiliate. On a $10M-equity deal, $200k of annual fees is a 2% headwind that the property must out-earn before the pref clock even starts. Always compute: total fees over the hold ÷ equity. Past ~15%, the GP wins even when you don't.

Reading a real deal like an examiner

Take any syndication PPM and run this sequence. One: find the pref, is it compounding or simple, cumulative or resetting? A "10% pref" that doesn't compound and isn't cumulative is quietly worth far less. Two: find the catch-up. 100% catch-up gets the GP to 20% fast; "50% catch-up" is friendlier to LPs; "no catch-up" (rare, LP-friendly) means the GP only earns on dollars above the pref. Three: map the hurdle schedule against the projected IRR, sponsors love projecting returns that land just above the tier where their promote steepens. Four: check whether the pref applies to invested capital or committed capital, and whether refinancing proceeds return capital (shrinking the pref base) or count as profit (inflating the promote). Five: find the clawback and its security. Every one of these is a lever that moves tens of thousands of dollars per $100k invested, and none of them appear in the marketing email.

Spot the Trick

Two deals both advertise "8% pref, 80/20 split." Deal A: pref compounds, European waterfall, promote on profits only. Deal B: simple pref, deal-by-deal promote, refi proceeds counted as profit, 2% acquisition fee, affiliated property manager. On identical 14% gross returns, LPs in Deal A net ≈ 12.1%; LPs in Deal B net ≈ 9.8%. Same headline. A quarter of the return moved through the fine print.

Why this module matters beyond real estate

This exact machinery, pref, catch-up, carry, clawback, runs private equity, venture capital, hedge fund incentive fees, and infrastructure funds. Real estate is simply where individual investors meet it first, in $25k crowdfunding slices with the same documents as $50M institutional checks. Master the waterfall once and you can price sponsor alignment anywhere. The next module applies it to PE and VC funds, where the same tiers wear different names.
