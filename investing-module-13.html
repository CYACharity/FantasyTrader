Capital Allocation: Dividends, Buybacks & ROIC

Long-Term Investing Advanced 5 min read

The CEO's real job

A mature company generates a river of free cash, and every dollar must go through one of five doors: reinvest in the business, acquire, buy back stock, pay dividends, or repay debt. Over a decade, the pattern of those choices — capital allocation — swamps almost everything else about the company. Buffett's observation: after ten years, a CEO who retains 10% of earnings annually has allocated more than half of all capital in the business. Most CEOs rise through operations or sales and have never been trained for this; the market pays fortunes to the few who are good at it.

The capital allocation decision tree — run every year, with compounding consequences.

The ranking rule is not controversial, just rarely followed: reinvest while ROIC exceeds the cost of capital; buy back stock when it trades below conservative intrinsic value; pay dividends when neither is true; acquire almost never at premiums; and repay debt when survival odds are being purchased cheaply. What managers actually do: acquire at 30% premiums near cycle tops (empire = pay), buy back the most stock at peak prices (2007's record buybacks preceded 2008; 2021's preceded 2022) and the least at bottoms, and treat the dividend as sacred even when it's funded with debt.

Buyback math, without the ideology

Buybacks are neither the fraud critics claim nor the gift promoters claim — they are simply an investment in your own stock, exactly as good as the price paid. Mechanics: a company worth $10B with 1B shares buys back 100M shares. Share count drops to 900M; each remaining share now claims 11.1% more of the business. If the company paid less than intrinsic value per share, remaining holders got richer; if it overpaid, wealth transferred from holders to sellers. That's the whole theory. The corollary: a company at 30× earnings buying back stock earns a 3.3% return on that "investment" — often while its own bonds yield more, which is arithmetic malpractice.

The Two Faces, Numerically

AutoZone: retired ~85% of its shares over 25 years at sensible prices — EPS compounded ~19%/yr on ~7% revenue growth; the stock 100×ed. That's buybacks as compounding machine. Counter-case: GE spent ~$40B+ on buybacks in 2015-17 at $25-30, then nearly collapsed and sold shares lower — buybacks as wealth incinerator. The tool was identical. The prices weren't.

Check two tells in any buyback story. Net vs gross: companies announcing $10B of buybacks while issuing $6B of stock comp are running a treadmill, not returning capital — track share count, not announcements. Funding: buybacks from free cash flow are a choice; buybacks from debt at cycle peaks are leverage wearing a shareholder-friendly costume.

Dividend quality, measured like a bond

A dividend is a promise attached to no contract, so underwrite it like a creditor. FCF payout ratio (dividends ÷ free cash flow — NOT earnings): under 60% has room to grow; over 90% is a promise one recession from breaking. Balance-sheet room: net debt/EBITDA above 3× means the dividend competes with lenders. History under fire: did it survive 2008 and 2020? The market's verdict system is severe — cuts are usually announced AFTER the stock has fallen 40%+, because dividend investors are the last to be told. And beware the yield trap you already know from REITs: an 9% yield on a melting business isn't income, it's a partial refund of your own capital with extra steps.

Total Shareholder Yield

The cleanest single lens: (dividends + net buybacks + net debt paydown) ÷ market cap. A company yielding 2% in dividends, retiring 3% of shares annually, and deleveraging 1% has a 6% total yield with growth optionality — quietly better than an 8% dividend funded by borrowing. This metric also exposes fake return-of-capital: heavy dividends PLUS heavy share issuance nets out to marketing.

Reading management like a capital allocator

The disclosure diet: read the last five years of shareholder letters and count how often capital allocation is discussed with numbers versus adjectives. Check insider ownership (allocators who own 10% behave differently from those with 0.1% and option grants). Compare stated hurdle rates to realized ROIC on past acquisitions — most companies never report deal outcomes, which is itself the answer. The elite tell: managers who buy back aggressively at lows and go quiet at highs (Berkshire, AutoZone, Exor) versus those who do the reverse (most of the index). You are not buying a stock; you are hiring an allocator and handing them your share of the river. Interview accordingly.
