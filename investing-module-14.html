M&A, Deal Math & Corporate Actions

Long-Term Investing Advanced 5 min read

When companies buy companies

Roughly $3-4 trillion of M&A happens in a normal year, and the evidence is settled and awkward: most acquisitions destroy acquirer value. Study after study puts the failure rate at 60-80%, and the reason is structural — buyers pay a 20-40% control premium up front against synergies that arrive late, half-sized, or never. Understanding deal mechanics matters even if you never touch merger arb, because sooner or later a company you own will be the buyer (usually bad), the target (usually great), or the neighbor repriced by the deal.

Deal currency shapes everything. Cash deals: the target's stock pins near the offer; your upside is capped, your remaining risk is deal completion. Stock deals: you're being paid in the acquirer's shares, so the "price" floats — and the acquirer's falling stock on announcement (the standard reaction) quietly shrinks your payout. The announcement-day pattern is one of the most reliable in finance: target up to just below the offer, acquirer down 2-8% as the market prices the premium paid, the integration risk, and the historical base rate above.

The spread is a probability meter

Anatomy of a cash deal: the discount to the offer price is the market's live estimate of completion risk.

Target trades at $59.90 against a $62 cash offer: a 3.5% gross spread. Annualized over an expected 7-month close: ~6%. That spread compresses as approvals land and blows out on regulator noise — watch it like a probability gauge. Extract the market's implied odds: if the deal breaks, the stock falls back toward its $45 standalone value. Implied probability of closing = (59.90 − 45) ÷ (62 − 45) ≈ 88%. When YOU think odds are 95% and the market prices 80%, that gap is a position; when a spread looks juicy for no visible reason, the market knows something about the FTC's mood that you don't. Recent history is a museum of both: wide "obviously fine" spreads that closed (paying arbs handsomely) and tight "done deals" that died by lawsuit.

Expected Value Discipline

Buy the target at $59.90. Close (est. 90%): +$2.10. Break (10%): −$14.90. EV = 0.9(2.10) − 0.1(14.90) = +$0.40 per share — positive but skinny, and one mis-estimated probability flips it negative. This shape (small gains, occasional cliffs) is why arb is a profession with risk systems rather than a hobby.

Spinoffs: the corporate action worth studying

The reverse deal — a company splitting in two — has the OPPOSITE empirical record: spun-off entities have historically beaten the market in their first two years (the classic Cusatis/Miles/Woolridge study found double-digit outperformance, echoed since). The mechanics create the anomaly: index funds and large holders receive small spinco shares they're often forbidden or unmotivated to hold, so the first weeks see valuation-blind selling; management of the spinco, newly incentivized with its own stock, stops being a neglected division. Joel Greenblatt built a career on exactly this garbage-collection dynamic. The playbook: read the Form 10, note where incentives land, let the forced selling exhaust itself (typically weeks, not days), and judge the spinco as a fresh business nobody has bothered to model yet.

The rest of the corporate-action zoo

Tender offers: a company or raider offers to buy shares at a premium, often with proration — if 80% of holders tender into an offer for 25% of shares, each seller moves only ~31% of their stake at the premium; the arithmetic of proration is routinely misjudged by retail holders. Rights offerings: existing holders may buy new shares at a discount — decline and you're diluted; the rights themselves have calculable value that many holders let expire (a genuine free-money leak). Splits change nothing fundamental, though inclusion effects and attention flows are real for a week. Special dividends pull value forward and drop the price by the payout on the ex-date — anyone "buying for the special dividend" the day before is buying their own money back, minus tax. Across all of them, one discipline: read the actual filing (8-K, S-4, Form 10), because the press release is written by the deal's marketers and the filing is written by its lawyers.

Portfolio Rules of Engagement

When your holding is ACQUIRED for cash at a fat premium: selling into the spread and redeploying usually beats waiting months for the last 2%. When your holding is the ACQUIRER of a mega-deal at a 35% premium: the base rates say downgrade your thesis. When a SPINOFF lands in your account: don't auto-sell with the crowd — that reflex is the other side's edge. And treat serial acquirers' "adjusted" EPS with the suspicion the base rates have earned.
